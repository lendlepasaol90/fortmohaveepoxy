Why coat an industrial slab

Uncoated warehouse concrete slowly wears itself out. Forklift and pallet jack wheels abrade the surface, releasing fine dust that settles on inventory, equipment and anything with a moving part. Spills soak in and stain. Joints break down at their edges until wheels start to bang across them, damaging both the floor and the equipment.

A coating system stops that cycle. It seals and hardens the surface so it no longer dusts. It resists oil, hydraulic fluid and cleaning chemicals. It makes the floor brighter — a light-colored coating can noticeably improve visibility in a building with limited windows. And it gives you a surface you can mark with aisles, walkways and hazard zones that stay visible.

Joints and cracks: the hidden weak points

In a busy warehouse, the floor usually fails at its joints before anywhere else. Saw-cut control joints and construction joints are edges, and edges break down under hard wheels. Once a joint starts to spall, every forklift crossing hits it harder, and the damage accelerates.

We repair failed joint edges with high-strength repair material, then fill joints with a semi-rigid filler that supports wheels while allowing small movements. Cracks are routed and filled. Damaged areas are rebuilt before coating so the finished floor is flat and continuous. This work often makes as much difference to the life of the floor as the coating itself.

System options for industrial floors

For buildings where a coating is not practical, densifying and sealing the concrete is another option. It reduces dust and improves cleanability without a film on top. We can discuss the trade-offs during a walkthrough.

Moderate use

  • Storage, light assembly, hangars
  • Two- to three-coat epoxy system
  • Urethane or polyaspartic topcoat
  • Optional light texture

Heavy use

  • Forklift aisles, fabrication, loading
  • High-build or broadcast epoxy
  • Aggregate for slip resistance
  • Heavier topcoat for abrasion

Line striping and safety marking

Clear floor markings make a warehouse safer and easier to run. We stripe forklift aisles, pedestrian walkways, staging areas, loading dock zones and hazard areas, using durable coatings that bond to the floor system rather than paint that wears away in months.

Common layouts use yellow for traffic aisles, white for storage and equipment locations, and red or black-and-yellow patterns for hazards and keep-clear areas. We can follow your existing layout or help plan a new one.

Heat inside metal buildings

Many industrial buildings in the valley are uninsulated metal structures, and in summer the air and slab inside can be extremely hot. That affects coating work the same way it does in a garage, only on a larger scale. We schedule installation for cooler hours, use products formulated for higher temperatures and, on large floors, increase crew size to keep each pour moving.

Heat also affects how the floor performs long term. A light-colored, UV-stable topcoat near open bay doors resists discoloration, and a flexible base can help with the expansion and contraction of large slabs. See polyurea floor coatings for more on flexible systems.

Hangar floors

Aircraft hangars benefit from coated floors in particular. A light, glossy surface reflects light up under wings and into engine compartments, making inspection and maintenance easier. Fuel, hydraulic fluid and oil wipe up instead of soaking in. Small dropped parts are easier to spot. And a clean floor reduces the risk of debris being picked up by propellers.

Hangar systems typically use chemical-resistant epoxy with a polyaspartic or urethane topcoat. Tie-down points and floor drains are detailed carefully, and textured areas can be added where people work.

Maintenance and long-term care

Auto scrubbers with soft pads and neutral cleaners keep coated industrial floors clean. Sweeping regularly to remove grit greatly extends the life of the topcoat. Spills should be cleaned up promptly, especially aggressive chemicals.

Forklift traffic will eventually wear the topcoat in the busiest lanes. Scheduled recoats — typically limited to those lanes — keep the floor protected without shutting down the whole building. For customer-facing commercial areas, see commercial epoxy flooring.

Dust control during preparation

Grinding and shot blasting a large industrial slab can generate a lot of dust if it is not controlled. Every piece of our preparation equipment is connected to industrial dust collection, and we contain the work area when inventory or equipment remains in the building. That protects stock, machinery and people working nearby.

Where the building stays in operation, we coordinate the sequence so preparation in one area does not interfere with work in another, and we clean each section before coating it.

Thinking about the life of the floor

A warehouse floor is a long-term asset. When comparing systems, it helps to think in terms of how long the floor will last before it needs maintenance and what that maintenance involves. A slightly heavier system with good joint repairs may cost more initially but go far longer before lanes need recoating. A lighter system may be the right answer for a storage building with little traffic.

We will explain the expected maintenance for each option so you can choose based on the full picture, not just the first invoice.

Loading docks and transitions

Loading docks take more abuse than almost any other part of a warehouse. Dock plates slam down, trucks bring in grit and water, and pallet jacks make sharp turns at the threshold. Concrete at dock edges is often chipped or spalled. We rebuild those edges with high-strength repair material and use heavier, textured systems in the dock area to resist wear and improve traction.

Transitions between coated and uncoated areas, or between different systems, are finished with a clean, saw-cut edge or a tapered detail so wheels roll smoothly from one surface to the next.

Brightness and energy

A light-colored, glossy floor reflects a significant amount of light compared with bare gray concrete. In large buildings, that can make spaces feel brighter and help workers see better without adding fixtures. It is one of the less obvious benefits of coating an industrial floor, and one that many facility managers notice immediately.

Racking and anchored equipment

Pallet racking, machinery and other anchored equipment complicate any coating project. Where racking can be unloaded and moved, coating underneath gives the best result. Where it cannot, we coat neatly around base plates and anchors and seal the edges. Those choices get settled at the site visit, well before the crew arrives.

Warehouse Floor Coatings: common questions

Can you coat while the warehouse is operating?

Yes, with phasing. We work in sections, often at night or on weekends, so inventory and equipment can be moved around the active area.

Will the coating handle forklifts?

Yes, when the system is chosen for the load and traffic. Heavy forklift areas get thicker builds and harder topcoats.

What happens to our existing joints?

Damaged joint edges are repaired and joints are filled with a semi-rigid filler so wheels roll smoothly across them.

Do you install floor striping?

Yes. We stripe aisles, walkways, staging areas and hazard zones using durable coatings.

Is densifying an option instead of coating?

For some buildings, yes. Densifying and sealing reduces dust without a film coating. We will compare both approaches for your space.

Can you coat a hangar with aircraft inside?

We typically ask for the hangar to be cleared for coating. For large facilities, we can phase the work so aircraft can be moved between completed sections.
