What a garage floor goes through here

Ask ten people in Fort Mohave what their garage holds and you will get ten different answers. One has a pickup and a bass boat. Another keeps two personal watercraft on a double trailer and a golf cart in the side bay. A third has turned the space into a workshop with a drill press, a welder and a chest freezer full of fish. Very few garages along the river are used only to park a sedan.

All of that activity lands on a concrete slab that was poured to be structural, not to be pretty or easy to clean. Plain concrete is porous. It drinks in motor oil, transmission fluid and the sunscreen that drips off a wet life jacket. It releases a fine powder as its surface wears. And in a place where the summer air can hold well above 110 degrees for weeks, a slab facing west can get hot enough to soften any coating that was not designed for it.

A professionally installed garage system addresses each of those problems. It seals the pores so spills sit on top until you wipe them up. It locks down the dusty surface. And when it is built and topcoated correctly, it handles hot tires, dropped tools and trailer jacks without lifting, chipping or turning yellow.

Systems we install in garages

Most garage projects use one of four approaches. The right one depends on your slab and your habits; this overview shows how they differ before we visit.

Full-broadcast flake

A colored base coat is covered edge to edge with vinyl chips, then sealed with a clear top. It hides dust, sand and water spots, offers a slight texture and is the most forgiving everyday finish. Decorative flake has its own page.

Solid color

A pigmented build in gray, tan or a custom shade with a gloss or satin top. Clean and bright, good for workshops and modern garages, and easy to mark with parking lines or borders.

Polyaspartic fast-cure

Polyaspartic base and topcoats cure in hours rather than days and resist UV yellowing. A strong choice when the garage needs to be back in use quickly. See polyaspartic coatings.

Polyurea base with flake

A flexible, high-bond polyurea base under a flake broadcast and polyaspartic top. It tolerates slab movement and heavy use, and suits shops and toy garages. See polyurea coatings.

Preparation is where floors succeed or fail

If you have seen a garage coating peeling in sheets or lifting in two neat rectangles where the tires sit, you have seen a preparation failure. Most of those floors were acid-etched or simply cleaned and then rolled. Acid barely touches a dense, steel-troweled slab, and it does nothing at all to builder sealers or old paint. The coating ends up resting on a smooth, weak surface, and the first hot tire pulls it loose.

We grind every garage with planetary diamond equipment connected to a dust extractor. Grinding removes the thin, weak top layer of the slab along with any sealer, paint or contamination, and leaves a profile with enough texture for the base coat to soak in and grip. Edges and corners that the big machine cannot reach are ground by hand.

Once the slab is open, we deal with damage. Cracks are cut open and filled with a rigid or semi-rigid repair material depending on whether they are still moving. Spalled areas near the garage door are rebuilt. Pitting is filled and ground flush so it does not show through a solid-color finish. Only then does the first coat go down.

Our installation, step by step

  1. 1

    Empty and protect

    The garage is cleared. We mask the walls, the door to the house, cabinet bases and anything that cannot be moved.

  2. 2

    Grind and vacuum

    Diamond grinding opens the surface; the dust extractor and a final vacuum leave the slab clean and ready.

  3. 3

    Repair

    Cracks, pits, spalls and failed joints are filled and ground smooth. Moisture readings are taken if anything looks suspicious.

  4. 4

    Base coat

    A tinted base coat is spread and back-rolled into the open concrete. This layer creates the bond.

  5. 5

    Color or flake

    Flake is broadcast to full coverage, or a solid build coat goes on. Excess chips are scraped and vacuumed once set.

  6. 6

    Clear topcoat

    A UV-stable polyaspartic or urethane top seals the floor. Anti-slip grit can be added for wet areas or ramps.

  7. 7

    Walkthrough

    Together we look over the finished floor, and you get clear times for foot traffic, parking and moving things back.

Boats, trailers and toy garages

Living close to the Colorado River means a lot of garages hold something with a hull or a hitch. Those floors deal with a few things a standard car garage does not. Trailer tongue jacks and stabilizer pads concentrate the weight of a boat or camper onto a few square inches. Wet hulls and trailers drip lake and river water that leaves mineral spots. And repeated trips in and out with sandy tires wear traffic lanes into softer finishes.

For these spaces we usually suggest a thicker build and a full flake broadcast, which camouflages spotting and adds grip. Placing a jack pad or small steel plate under tongue jacks and stands spreads the load and protects any floor for years. If you hose the boat down inside, we can add a slightly heavier grit to the topcoat in that bay.

Side-by-sides and ATVs bring their own challenge: knobby tires that track in gravel and dirt. A flake floor hides that grit well between cleanings, and the sealed surface lets you sweep or blow it out the door instead of grinding it into raw concrete.

  • Heavier film build in trailer and jack locations
  • Full flake broadcast to hide water spots and sand
  • Optional grit in wash-down bays
  • Coating carried up stem walls so hose water cannot soak the base

Heat, sunlight and open garage doors

Summer is the main event in Fort Mohave. Slab temperatures in a closed garage can stay high well into the night, and an open door on a west-facing home lets the afternoon sun bake the front few feet of floor. Two problems follow. First, coatings react faster on a hot surface, which shortens the time installers have to spread and level them. Second, ordinary epoxy darkens and yellows under UV light over time.

We handle the first problem with scheduling and material choice. In warm months we begin early in the morning, measure slab temperature before mixing, and mix in smaller batches so each section is worked well within its window. Where it helps, we use slower-reacting formulas made for high temperatures.

The second is solved by the topcoat. Every garage system we install is finished with an aliphatic polyaspartic or urethane topcoat that holds its color in sunlight. That keeps a light gray floor from drifting toward amber and protects the color and flake beneath it.

Choosing colors that work in a dusty climate

The color of a garage floor has a lot to do with how clean it looks between washes. Very dark solids show every speck of tan dust. Very light solids show tire marks. The blends that look best day to day in this valley mix several tones — a warm tan, a mid gray, a little black and a little white — so that sand, dust and small marks blend into the pattern.

It also helps to think about the rest of the space. Cabinets, the garage door and the wall color all sit next to the floor. Lighter floors bounce more light around and make a garage feel larger, which is helpful in spaces with limited windows. We bring physical samples to every estimate because colors look very different under fluorescent tubes than they do on a phone screen.

Professional system or DIY kit?

Kits are inexpensive for a reason. They are fine for a light-use shed, but in a garage that sees heat, vehicles and trailers they rarely last. The cost of grinding off a failed kit later often erases the savings.

Retail kit

  • Acid etch or cleaner instead of grinding
  • Thin, single-coat film
  • Standard epoxy that yellows in sun
  • Often lifts under hot tires in the first summer
  • Failed kits must be ground off before recoating

Professional system

  • Diamond-ground, repaired slab
  • Multi-coat build of meaningful thickness
  • UV-stable topcoat
  • Designed to resist hot-tire pickup
  • Written scope listing each layer

Caring for a coated garage

Maintenance is simple. Sweep or blow out dust and sand regularly; grit is what dulls a topcoat over time. Rinse or mop with a mild, pH-neutral cleaner as needed. Wipe up oil, brake fluid and battery acid promptly. Place mats or plates under kickstands, jack stands and trailer jacks. Avoid dragging sharp, heavy items across the floor without protection.

A well-installed floor will show wear first in the lanes where tires turn. If you ever want to refresh the shine, the topcoat can be lightly abraded and recoated without redoing the whole system.

Garage Floor Coatings: common questions

How soon can I park after the garage is coated?

With a polyaspartic top, foot traffic is usually fine that evening and vehicles can return in about two to four days, depending on temperature. Epoxy-based systems take longer, and we will give you exact times on the day.

Will hot tires lift the coating?

Hot-tire pickup happens when a coating is too thin or poorly bonded. Diamond grinding and a proper multi-coat build are what prevent it, and that is how every one of our garage floors is installed.

Can you coat over my existing garage paint?

No. Paint and old sealers have to be ground off completely, because a new coating can only be as well bonded as whatever it sits on.

What about cracks in my slab?

Cracks are opened and filled before coating. Some cracks in a slab will continue to move with temperature, and while a repair keeps them sealed, a hairline may still reappear over time.

Do you coat the stem walls?

Yes. Carrying the coating a few inches up the perimeter walls creates a clean edge and keeps water from soaking under the floor when you hose it out.

Is a flake floor slippery?

Flake floors have a natural texture that helps with grip. For extra traction near entries or wash areas, we add a fine anti-slip aggregate to the topcoat.
